How the answer key is made

  • Scale: from two tape measurements (holder 21 cm tall; controller 5.5 cm thick). The combined scan, fitted with the holder at free scale, agrees within 1.2%.
  • Two passes, one controller: face-up and standing scans aligned by a similarity ICP over a rotation search (0.5 mm RMSE), pen box and paper removed, colors matched on the overlap, Poisson-meshed.
  • Ground truth: both assets registered into the combined scan (holder 2.8 mm RMSE, controller 1.0 mm). The dark, glossy figure limits this to a few mm, so the 9.1 mm settle error is an upper bound.
  • Leg gap: Poisson had filled in the space between the figure's legs. From the front, the dark legs stand against bright paper, so each photo votes for the 3D points it sees background through; those are cut out of the mesh (before/after). Below 35 mm the gap is seen only against the black base, so its outline is extended down from there. carve.py

Dexterous grasping: Shadow Hand

The 24-joint Shadow Hand (MuJoCo Menagerie) lifts scanned objects and survives a ±2 cm / 3 Hz shake. The camera picks the side where the thumb is visible.

A benchmark for comparing assets, not a grasp planner: 90 random, unoptimized power grasps for each of 8 benchmark assets and 2 collision variants (1,440 trials, ~1 min on 16 cores). Convex decomposition holds more of them than one hull on the six hand-sized objects (23.5% vs 18.9%), and grasps from above beat grasps from the side (34% vs 4%). The best of these grasps are stored in the assets (see Assets that know what they don’t know).

Five fingers: Shadow Hand places the controller (MuJoCo, then Isaac Sim and Unreal)

The Panda's job (chapter 02) with a floating Shadow Hand. A pinch fails: the 243 g controller hangs 6 cm off it, beyond what the fingertip servos (1 N·m) can hold. So the hand spans the controller's width level with its center of mass (thumb on one side, middle and ring fingers on the other), lifts, yaws palm-down, then tilts upright about the controller's center: blended moves with minimum-jerk timing, stopping only to grip, to re-aim and to let go. Four fixes, each from a failed trial: span instead of pinch; curl the middle finger so the grip isn't two points it can spin about; two separate turns (one slerp swung the palm up); tray out of the forearm's way. 12 trials (±2 cm / ±10°): open loop 12/12 held, 13.1 mm / 22° (the grip rolls it in the hand); re-aimed from the in-hand pose before inserting (read from the simulation, standing in for a wrist camera): 12/12, 6.4 mm / 6° (both, side by side). One convex hull: 0/12. No arm, so reach isn't checked. Scale: the hand is the real robot's size, which Shadow specifies as “similar in shape and size to a typical male hand” (201 mm wrist to fingertip, 84 mm palm; an average man's hand is about 190 mm); the big cylinder is its motor-filled forearm. All three videos use a 57 mm lens: at 24 mm the hand, always nearest the camera, looked oversized. scripts/demo_hand_place.py; plan, timing and cameras shared by all three engines: scripts/hand_motion.py

Isaac Sim 6 (PhysX): the same experiment. The same Menagerie hand (Isaac's MJCF importer), scanned USD assets, per-seed poses and waypoints; PhysX simulates every contact, and the re-aimed run measures the controller in PhysX. Re-aimed 12/12, 6.3 mm / 5°; open loop 12/12, 19.5 mm / 27°; one convex hull 0/12. One fix needed: targets every physics substep (see Lessons). scripts/isaac_hand_place.py

Train on synthetic images, test on real photos

A Faster R-CNN detector trained only on Isaac Sim renders of our scanned assets, run on the 67 real Buddha photos it never saw. Green: the Buddha. Red: false detections. Trained without distractors, the detector calls markers and lab clutter "mugs".

Each frame randomizes which of the 9 assets appear and where, the table, lights and camera, plus unlabeled distractors. 1,000 labeled frames in 3.5 min (900 for training, 100 held out).

Closing the loop: see it, locate it, pick it

In MuJoCo, the lion figure lands at a random pose among other scans (our scanned mug is now a distractor). Two RGB-D scene cameras → the synthetic-trained detector finds it (left, green) → depth inside the box is fitted with the scanned mesh → the Panda pinches the torso from above, across the body, at the estimated pose. 10/10 picks, median pose error 0.6 mm / 0.3°. Unlike the mug (whose handle can hide from both cameras), the lion's head and tail make yaw unambiguous, and the grasp needs it: closing along the body instead of across it would miss. The scan is used three times: training images, pose model, and physics.

Verify

How accurate must a scan be?

93 simulated Panda picks. The robot always plans with the nominal scan; the simulated "real" mug differs. A: collision geometry decides it. One hull or a bounding box (library variants) fails at every friction. B: doubling the mass breaks the grip, so weigh your object. C: size errors within ±5% are tolerated even with ±8 mm grasp error; the ColorChecker's ≤1.43% scale error is well inside. A real mug 10–15% smaller than the scan makes the fingers miss the rim. Grip force ~9 N (a real Panda hand: up to 70 N).

Rendering: Isaac Sim and Unreal Engine

Same USD files in Unreal Engine 5.8: Lumen + MegaLights

The scene is one USD stage (unreal_layout.py): the scan assets by reference, a wood table, a key light and two strings of fairy lights, 80 shadow-casting point lights. The lion (a Google Scanned Objects scan) is the in-focus hero; the phone-scanned holder and controller stand at the back, out of focus, where their noisier phone-camera detail doesn't carry the shot. Unreal's USD importer brings it in unchanged, apart from one look override in the scene layer (the holder's paint is satin, not the generic plastic preset). Lumen with hardware ray tracing gives the bounce light; MegaLights gives every bulb its own soft shadow. 1440p, Movie Render Queue; the whole build and render is scripted (unreal_render.py). 10-second orbit.

What MegaLights buys: 8.8 vs 14.8 ms GPU per frame

One camera, three renderers. Times are the median GPU frame at 1080p on an RTX 4090 (Unreal's CSV profiler, 600 frames per run, median of 3 runs: 7.8–9.2 ms with MegaLights, 14.8–15.1 ms without). Without MegaLights, each shadowed light is drawn separately: shadow maps plus per-light shading. With it, the lights are sampled stochastically, ray-traced and denoised: about 40% less GPU time, and a slightly closer match to Unreal's own path tracer (PSNR 24.8 vs 23.3 dB against 4,096 samples per pixel). Real-time still isn't the reference: contact shadows and bulb bloom differ.

Scanned assets in Isaac Sim 6 RTX

Offline path tracing, 1,024 samples per pixel, AI denoiser, 6 bounces. HDRI dome + area key light, NVIDIA MDL oak table, physically correct 50 mm f/5.6 depth of field. Assets carry PBR look presets from their material (the speckled mug's glaze is a clearcoat over a rough body) and baked 2K albedo + normal maps. Real-time vs offline path tracing.

High-to-low normal map bake

4.3M-triangle scan → 50k triangles. Left: vertex colors only. Right: the scan's detail baked into a tangent-space normal map, under grazing light. A cage-style test ignores source points facing away (the far side of thin walls), and hard edges stay hard: averaged normals across a fitted base's 90° rim once streaked its wall.

Look-dev against a real photo

Render from the photo's solved camera. 63% of the silhouette lies within 3 px of a photo edge (median 2 px), which checks geometry, scale and camera at once. After white balance, color is median ΔE2000 6.4; the rest is the unknown room lighting.

Assets that know what they don't know

Scan-to-sim tools usually output one confident mesh. These assets also store what the camera actually saw, what was guessed, how to hold them, and how they really look: all inside the USD file, where any simulator can read it.

Uncertainty in the asset (Scan2UsdUncertaintyAPI)

Every vertex is labeled observed, inferred or sealed (no camera could see it). Mass, scale and friction become intervals, and a carved geometry hypothesis assumes unseen cavities are empty (a prior, not ray-based carving, which can't clear space no camera saw): bowl F-score 0.49 → 0.95. On solid objects, carving is worse, so "filled" stays the default. The bowl's interval misses (206–400 g vs 191 g).

Grasps in the asset (Scan2UsdGraspAPI)

A codeless USD schema stores grasp poses with their tested success rate per gripper and collision variant. scan2usd grasps lists them. Isaac Sim reads the best stored grasp: the pick succeeds (5.5 mm placement error) with convex decomposition and fails with one hull, as in MuJoCo. Physics randomized within the asset's intervals also predicts real grasp success better than the nominal asset (Brier error 0.12 → 0.05; lower is better).

Hybrid asset: Gaussian splat for looks, hulls for physics

A splat trained on the same photos (Brush) becomes a UsdVol.ParticleField3DGaussianSplat under the rigid body, in an appearance variant set. The mesh's silhouette masks every training photo, so the splat learns only the object. On 8 real photos it never saw: ΔE2000 6.9 → 3.6, PSNR 19.7 → 27.0 dB, SSIM 0.64 → 0.78. Closest views are softer than the mesh.

Splats ride on PhysX

Isaac Sim 6, RTX real-time path tracing: splat Buddha and mugs fall on their convex hulls. An inset copy of the mesh, hidden from the camera, casts the shadows (splat_proxy). Gaussians receive shadows in RTX, so a proxy at the surface turned them black. The offline path tracer renders splats dark; real-time mode is fine. Video.

Franka picks a splat mug

Same plan, the mug drawn by its splat: lifted 14.5 cm, placed 1.8 cm from the target. The mask ablation matters here: trained on raw photos, the cropped mug splat is see-through (PSNR 21.6); with mesh masks, 28.9 dB. Mug comparison · mesh vs splat vs proxy.

Notes

Learn

Photogrammetry with COLMAP · photos → points → mesh

Structure from motion matches features across photos and solves every camera's position. Multi-view stereo then estimates depth per pixel and fuses millions of colored points. A Poisson solve turns the points into a closed surface.

reconstruct.py · table plane → Z-up; object kept as the densest footprint above the table

Accuracy, completeness, F-score · how reconstruction is graded

Accuracy: how far the built surface is from the truth. Completeness: how much of the truth was captured. F-score at τ combines both: the share of each surface within τ (2 mm here) of the other. These are the standard metrics of the DTU and Tanks & Temples benchmarks.

evaluate.py · yaw search + ICP alignment, then point-to-surface distances

Normal maps & PBR · the game-art pipeline, for scans

Real-time engines render a light mesh and fake the fine detail: a normal map stores, per texel, how the dense surface tilts relative to the low-poly one (in the "tangent space" of its UV layout). PBR materials describe surfaces physically (base color, roughness, metalness, index of refraction, a clearcoat layer for glazes), so they look right under any light. A path tracer then simulates the light itself. For robotics, the same assets give physics a light collision mesh and give cameras realistic images.

bake.bake_normal_map · usd_io.LOOKS · scripts/isaac_pathtrace.py

Color calibration · physically based calibration, for scans

A 3×3 matrix maps the camera's colors of known chart patches to their true values in linear light; it's then applied to the scan's albedo. ΔE2000 ≈ 1 is just noticeable.

color.py

Metric scale from the chart · no ruler needed

Photogrammetry alone can't tell a mug from a giant mug: its scale is arbitrary. The ColorChecker's patches are a known distance apart (16 mm on the Mini), so the pipeline finds the chart in many photos, triangulates its 24 patch centers with the solved cameras, and reads off meters per unit. RANSAC drops misdetections. On the benchmark: 0.35–1.43% error.

chart.py · scan2usd scan --chart-pitch-mm 16

Convex decomposition · why the grasp works

Physics engines collide convex shapes quickly. One hull around a mug fills its cavity; CoACD splits it into pieces that keep the opening, so fingers and objects can go inside.

physics.py

Mass for real, holey scans · voxel fill

Mass needs a closed solid. Scans often have holes, so the pipeline voxelizes the surface, closes small gaps, and fills enclosed space; open cavities (bowls, cups) stay empty, as long as the reconstruction left them open (see the benchmark's sealed mug). Example: bowl 322 g (hull estimate) → 192 g.

physics.voxel_solid

Articulations in USD · lids, doors, laptops

Each part is its own rigid body; a UsdPhysics.RevoluteJoint joins them with a hinge axis and angle limits, under an ArticulationRootAPI so PhysX solves them as one mechanism.

articulation.py · scan2usd articulate

USD composition: payloads & variants · one library, many configurations

USD builds a scene from layers. A payload is a reference that loads on demand, so a 1,000-asset library opens instantly and loads only what a scene needs. A variant set is a named switch with alternative opinions inside, here collision and friction. Variants are stronger than payloads (the "LIVRPS" strength order), so the library can hide an asset's colliders and add simpler ones without editing the asset file. The same idea drives LODs and material looks in film and game pipelines.

library.py · scan2usd library · catalog

Synthetic data & sim-to-real · Replicator, domain randomization

Robots learn perception from labeled images. Simulators render perfect labels for free. The catch is the sim-to-real gap: a model that's perfect on renders can still fail on photos. Domain randomization varies everything that shouldn't matter (lights, colors, camera, junk objects) so the model learns only what does. Here, distractors cut false positives on real photos by 84%.

scripts/isaac_synthdata.py · scripts/train_detector.py

Gaussian splatting · a photo-real look from the same photos

Instead of a surface with a texture, a splat is a cloud of small, colored, semi-transparent 3D Gaussians, optimized until their renders match the photos. Each one stores its color as spherical harmonics, so it changes with the viewing angle (sheen, glaze). The result looks like the photos, but there's no surface, so a physics engine can't collide with it. OpenUSD 26.03 added a standard prim for them (ParticleField3DGaussianSplat). Here the mesh does the physics and also tells the splat trainer where the object is (alpha masks).

splat.py · scan2usd splat-data → Brush → scan2usd splat · scripts/isaac_splat.py

Uncertainty in an asset · intervals, not just values

A scan measures some things well (surface it saw from two angles), guesses others (a hole the mesher closed) and can't know some at all (the inside of a cup nobody photographed). Storing that with the asset lets a simulator randomize physics where the scan is unsure (domain randomization, but grounded in the measurement) and tells you which photos to take next. Custom properties live in a codeless USD schema: a plugin with only a plugInfo.json and a schema file, no compiled code, so Isaac Sim, usdview and Python all read it.

uncertainty.py · schemas.py · guide.py · scan2usd uncertainty | grasps | guide

Lessons

  1. Test on real data, not just synthetic. The detector scored a perfect 1.00 on held-out renders and still made 4.3 false detections per real photo. Unlabeled distractors in training cut that by 84%.
  2. Read the units, don't tune by eye. Depth of field was far too strong. USD camera lens values are in tenths of a scene unit, so on a meters stage "50" is a 5 m lens: same framing, 100× the aperture. Writing 0.05 gives a real 50 mm f/5.6 lens.
  3. Look at a cross-section before believing an explanation. The bowl's +82% volume looked like thick walls; slicing the solid showed an unseen underside filled into a pedestal. No line of sight, so carving can't fix it, but a stored "carved" hypothesis can.
  4. Robot models need realistic actuators. The stock Panda gripper squeezed a cup wall with ~0.5 N (MuJoCo) and ~2 N (Isaac), so the mug slipped in both; a real Franka hand reaches 70 N. A realistic drive made the same pick work in both engines.
  5. Don't teleport a hand. A mocap-driven forearm moves without velocity, and MuJoCo friction is velocity-based: 120 N of squeeze and the object stayed put. Welding a free forearm to the target fixed it.
  6. Visible isn't reconstructable. The capture guide aimed into the printed mug's white interior: in view, but nothing for stereo to match. Measuring the guide on three objects, not one, is what showed it.
  7. Let the mesh help the splat. Trained on raw photos, a splat spends its Gaussians on the background. Masking every photo with the mesh's silhouette raised held-out PSNR from 21.6 to 28.9 dB.
  8. Control at the physics rate. In Isaac Sim the hand dropped the controller at every contact stiffness I tried; the cause was the control loop. Kinematic targets set once per 60 Hz frame, with 8 PhysX substeps in between, move the hand in jolts. Setting them in a pre-step callback (every substep, as MuJoCo's loop did) took it from 0/4 to 12/12.
  9. Meshes need UVs, even for flat colors. In Unreal the robot hand rendered dark gray whatever color I gave it. The materials were right; the meshes, exported from the simulator, had no UVs, so Unreal's tangent frames were degenerate and even a flat normal map shaded them dark. A planar UV projection fixed it. (A first symptom, new shader permutations still compiling when Movie Render Queue started, hid this one.)
  10. A wide lens makes the nearest thing look big. The true-scale Shadow Hand looked oversized next to a holder I had measured by hand. The model was right; the camera was MuJoCo's default 45° view, a 24 mm lens, and the hand is always closest to it. The same shots on a 57 mm lens from farther back read at natural proportions.

Shoot

  • Start easy: a matte, textured object (wooden toy, sneaker, speckled mug). Dark, glossy or plain surfaces are the hardest; masking tape with pen marks helps inside plain cups.
  • 60–150 photos: walk around it in 3 rings with 60–80% overlap; keep object and lights still; lock exposure, white balance and focus; no flash.
  • Put a newspaper under the object and a ColorChecker beside it in a few frames, plus one close-up.

Measure, then run

  • Weigh it; --mass is in kilograms (360 g → --mass 0.36). A ruler measurement (mm) is optional if the ColorChecker is in the photos.
  • scan2usd scan photos\ -o out\ --chart-pitch-mm 16 --mass 0.36 --material ceramic (photos → USD in one step; measure your chart's patch spacing once)
  • scan2usd guide out\asset\<name>.usda --workspace out\colmap suggests extra shots.
